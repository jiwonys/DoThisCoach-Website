Sport-day training

How to Train Around Game Day Without Losing Progress

Most athletes plan training and competition separately, then discover the collision on the day. Planning the week backwards from the game solves almost all of it.

Published August 19, 2026Updated August 19, 202611 min read
The short answer

Work backwards from the game. Put the heaviest lower-body session on the day after competition, keep the two days before it light or upper-body focused, and count the game itself as hard training when you tally the week.

Who this plan is for

This is for recreational athletes in weekly leagues who also lift, and who are tired of choosing between the two.

  • League players with one or two fixed competition days.
  • Lifters whose program was written as if the calendar were empty.
  • Athletes who feel flat in competition and cannot work out why.
  • Coaches organizing training for adults who compete weekly.

Progression rules

In season, hold progress rather than chasing it. Out of season, push.

  1. In competition weeks, add load only when the previous week felt genuinely easy.
  2. Cut a set before cutting a session; frequency preserves more than volume does.
  3. Two games in one week means one gym session, not two.
  4. Return to normal progression during any break in the fixture list.

Weekly placement and recovery

  • Count the game in your weekly hard-session total. Most athletes undercount it badly.
  • The day before competition is the cheapest day to rest and the most expensive day to train hard.
  • Soreness on game day is a planning failure, not a badge.
  • Persistent fatigue across several weeks usually means total load is too high, not that you need a harder session.

Common programming questions

Can I lift on game day at all?

Yes, if it is short, upper-body focused, and hours before the game. Heavy legs belong on a different day.

What if I have two games a week?

Drop to one lower-body session, placed after the first game, and treat the second game as the week's other hard session.

Will two sessions a week be enough?

For an in-season athlete already competing, two well-placed sessions maintain and often still build strength.

Safety and limitations

Stop if an exercise produces sharp pain, dizziness, chest pain, or symptoms that feel unsafe. Competing on an injury is a decision for you and a qualified clinician, not a training plan.
