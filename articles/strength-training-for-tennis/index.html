Strength Training for Tennis: A Practical Weekly Plan

Tennis is rotational, one-sided, and full of hard stops. A useful gym plan trains the stopping and the rotating, not just the pushing.

Published August 19, 2026Updated August 19, 202612 min read
The short answer

Train two to three gym sessions per week: one lower-body strength day emphasising single-leg work and deceleration, one upper-body day emphasising pulling and shoulder capacity, and optionally one short rotational power day placed away from match play.

Who this plan is for

For club and recreational tennis players who want to play more without accumulating shoulder, hip, or ankle complaints.

  • Players adding court time and feeling it in the shoulder or hip.
  • Adults returning to tennis after a break.
  • Players whose gym work is all pressing and no pulling.
  • Anyone wanting more durable serving volume.

Weekly schedule

Court time is the priority. Place the heaviest lower-body day away from the most competitive match of the week.

The tennis strength plan

Leave two or three repetitions in reserve. The sport already supplies plenty of maximal effort.

Day 1 - Lower body and deceleration

Single-leg dominant, with landing quality trained deliberately.

More pulling than pressing, plus dedicated cuff capacity.

Short and fast. Stop as soon as speed drops.

Progression rules

Strength progresses by load; rotational power progresses by speed and quality.

  1. Add reps then load on the strength lifts.
  2. Stop any throw or hop set when output drops; slow reps train slowness.
  3. Increase court hours gradually, especially serving volume.
  4. Reduce the optional day in any week with a tournament.

Weekly placement and recovery

  • One-sided sport creates side-to-side differences. That is normal; large gaps are worth addressing.
  • Shoulder soreness after serving volume is common; pain during the serve is not.
  • Hip and adductor tightness often follows a jump in court time rather than a lack of stretching.
  • Persistent pain deserves assessment rather than a heavier warmup.

Common programming questions

Will lifting slow my swing?

No. Force production supports racquet speed. Fatigue is what slows a swing, which is a scheduling problem.

Should I train my non-dominant side equally?

Yes for general strength and durability. Perfect symmetry is not the goal, but large differences are worth reducing.

How close to a match can I lift?

Keep heavy lower-body work at least a day away. Light upper-body work the morning of a match is usually fine.

Safety and limitations

Stop for sharp pain, dizziness, chest pain, or symptoms that feel unsafe. Shoulder pain during serving, or hip pain that changes your movement, deserves clinical assessment.
