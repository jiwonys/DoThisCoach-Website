Training myths

Does Lifting Make You Slow? What the Evidence Actually Says

Strength training does not make athletes slow. Poorly organized training does, and so does carrying fatigue into the moments that demand speed.

Published August 19, 2026Updated August 19, 202610 min read
The short answer

No. Resistance training generally supports rather than harms speed and power, because force production underpins both. What does make athletes slow is chronic fatigue, training that replaces sprinting and jumping entirely, and gaining mass that the sport does not need.

Who this plan is for

This is for athletes who have been told that lifting will make them bulky, stiff, or slow, and who want a clear account of where that belief comes from.

  • Court and field athletes deciding whether to add strength work.
  • Parents and coaches who are unsure whether lifting is appropriate for a young athlete.
  • Lifters who have noticed their sprinting feel worse during a hard block.
  • Anyone weighing hypertrophy goals against sport performance.

The organizing principle is simple: train speed when fresh, train strength after, and never let the gym consume the qualities you actually compete with.

How to lift without dulling speed

The order matters more than the exercise selection. Fast work comes first, while you are fresh.

Speed-priority session

Short, sharp, and finished long before fatigue accumulates.

Progression rules

Speed qualities progress by quality, not accumulation. Strength progresses by load.

  1. Stop any sprint or jump set the moment output drops. A slow rep trains slowness.
  2. Progress strength lifts with the usual double progression: reps first, then the smallest load increase.
  3. Keep sprint volume low and recovery long. Two to three minutes between short sprints is normal.
  4. If sprint times or jump heights fall for two weeks, you are carrying too much fatigue, not too little strength.

Weekly placement and recovery

  • Speed is the first quality to disappear when sleep is short. Treat sleep as part of the program.
  • Do not judge a speed session by how tired it made you. Judge it by how fast it was.
  • Heavy lower-body work within a day of competition is the most common self-inflicted cause of feeling slow.
  • Unusual hamstring tightness before a speed session is a reason to reduce the session, not push through it.

Common programming questions

Will lifting make me bulky?

Meaningful mass gain requires a sustained calorie surplus and years of consistent training. Strength can improve substantially with very little change in body size.

Should young athletes lift?

Supervised resistance training with submaximal loads and good technique is broadly appropriate. Avoid maximum-effort singles and training to failure.

I feel slower during a heavy block. Is that normal?

Yes. Fatigue temporarily masks speed. It should return within a week of reducing volume, which is what a deload is for.

Safety and limitations

Sprinting and jumping carry injury risk when performed cold, fatigued, or in unsuitable footwear. Stop for sharp pain and seek assessment for any suspected muscle strain rather than training through it.
