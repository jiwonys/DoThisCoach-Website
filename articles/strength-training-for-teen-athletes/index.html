Strength Training for Teen Athletes: A Safe, Effective Starting Plan

Supervised resistance training is well established as safe for adolescents. The genuine risks are poor supervision and too much sport volume, not the weights themselves.

Published July 30, 2026Updated July 30, 202610 min read
The short answer

Start with two to three supervised sessions a week using moderate loads and higher reps, prioritising technique over load. The old claim that lifting stunts growth is not supported by evidence.

Who this plan is for

This is for teenage athletes and the parents and coaches around them. Supervision by a qualified adult is assumed throughout.

  • Athletes aged roughly thirteen and up starting structured training.
  • Parents worried about whether lifting is safe.
  • Coaches building an off-season program for a school team.
  • Teens playing multiple sports who need a manageable plan.

Weekly schedule

Two to three sessions a week, always around sport rather than on top of it. Total weekly load across sport and gym is what matters.

The teen athlete plan

Reps stay moderate to high and loads stay conservative. The goal for the first year is competent technique across every pattern, not a maximum.

Progression rules

Progress technique first, then reps, then load. A teenager who spends a year building clean patterns will out-lift one who chased numbers, within two years.

  1. Add load only when every set is technically clean at the top of the rep range.
  2. Keep increments small. Enthusiasm outpaces tissue readiness at this age more than at any other.
  3. Avoid maximal single-rep testing in the first year of training.
  4. Total load matters: if sport volume rises, gym volume comes down.

Weekly placement and recovery

  • Teenage athletes are usually under-slept, and sleep affects both performance and injury risk substantially.
  • Playing one sport year-round is associated with more overuse problems than playing several.
  • Growth spurts often bring temporary coordination and tendon issues; reduce load rather than pushing through.

Common programming questions

Does lifting stunt growth?

No. Reviews of supervised youth resistance training find no negative effect on growth, and the injury rate is lower than in most sports these athletes already play.

What age can you start?

Children can train with bodyweight and light loads well before adolescence, provided they can follow instructions and are supervised. Age matters less than maturity and coaching quality.

Should teens max out?

Not in the first year. Maximal attempts add risk without adding much information at a stage where technique is still developing.

Safety and limitations

Stop if an exercise produces sharp pain, dizziness, or chest pain. Adolescents should train under qualified supervision, and any pain near a growth plate, particularly at the knee, heel, or elbow, needs medical assessment rather than modified training.
