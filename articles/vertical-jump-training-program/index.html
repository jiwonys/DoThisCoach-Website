Power development

Vertical Jump Training Program: Strength, Plyometrics, and Landing

Jump height comes from force and the speed at which it is expressed. Strength raises the ceiling; plyometrics teach access to it; landing quality decides whether you keep training.

Published August 19, 2026Updated August 19, 202612 min read
The short answer

Train two strength sessions and one to two low-volume plyometric sessions per week. Keep jump contacts well under 100 per session, always train jumps fresh, and stop any set the moment jump quality drops.

Who this plan is for

For court and field athletes who want a higher jump and have at least a few months of consistent lifting behind them.

  • Volleyball, basketball, and court athletes wanting measurable jump gains.
  • Athletes who have trained jumps hard and stalled.
  • Lifters who are strong but jump poorly.
  • Anyone who has developed jumper's knee from unmanaged plyometric volume.

Weekly schedule

Jumping is a nervous-system quality. It belongs at the start of the session and the start of the week, not tacked onto the end of a tired day.

The jump program

Count contacts. The single most common mistake in jump training is doing far too many.

Day 1 - Plyometrics and heavy strength

Roughly 40 contacts, then strength. Stop jumping when height falls.

Progression rules

Strength progresses by load. Jumps progress by quality first, contacts second, and complexity last.

  1. Add load to the strength lifts using the usual double progression.
  2. Increase jump contacts by no more than roughly 20 percent per week.
  3. Add depth jumps only once landings are silent and stable from a low box.
  4. End any jump set when height or crispness drops. Fatigued jumping trains the wrong thing.

Weekly placement and recovery

  • Tendon adaptation lags muscle adaptation. Progress plyometric volume more slowly than it feels you could.
  • Front-of-knee pain that worsens across a block is the classic sign of too many contacts too soon.
  • Two hard jumping days per week is enough for almost everyone.
  • During competition weeks, matches replace one plyometric session rather than adding to it.

Common programming questions

How long before I see a change?

Athletes new to structured jump training often see measurable change in six to twelve weeks. Experienced jumpers progress more slowly.

Do I need depth jumps?

No. They are a useful advanced tool but carry more load per contact. Plenty of progress happens without them.

Should I jump every day?

No. Daily maximal jumping is one of the more reliable ways to develop tendon pain.

Safety and limitations

Plyometric training loads tendons heavily. Stop for sharp or worsening joint pain and seek assessment for pain that persists beyond a few days rather than training through it.
