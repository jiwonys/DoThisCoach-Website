Single-Leg Training: How to Find and Fix Side-to-Side Imbalances

Small side-to-side differences are normal and not worth chasing. Large ones show up as pain or performance limits, and single-leg work is how you close them.

Published August 2, 2026Updated August 2, 20269 min read
The short answer

Test with a single-leg exercise and compare reps at the same load. Differences beyond roughly ten percent are worth addressing: start each set with the weaker side and match the stronger side to its reps.

Who this plan is for

This is for people who suspect one side is doing more of the work, whether from an old injury, a one-sided sport, or a nagging joint.

  • Athletes in rotational or one-sided sports.
  • Anyone returning from a lower-limb injury.
  • Lifters whose barbell drifts or whose hips shift out of a squat.
  • People with a knee or hip that only ever bothers one side.

Weekly schedule

Add single-leg work to your existing plan rather than replacing bilateral lifting. Two exposures a week is enough to close most gaps in a couple of months.

Test, then train

The rule that does the work: always start with the weaker side, and give the stronger side only as many reps as the weaker side managed.

The test

One session. Same load, both sides, taken close to failure. Record the reps.

Progression rules

The weaker side sets the ceiling for both. This is what actually closes a gap; matching sides by load alone does not.

  1. Always begin every set with the weaker side.
  2. Give the stronger side exactly the reps the weaker side achieved, not more.
  3. Progress load only when the weaker side reaches the top of the rep range.
  4. Retest after six to eight weeks. Once inside roughly ten percent, return to normal training and stop tracking it.

Weekly placement and recovery

  • A gap that will not close despite consistent work may be structural or the residue of an old injury; that is a clinician's question.
  • Do not remove bilateral lifting to chase an imbalance. Squats and deadlifts are not the cause.
  • Some asymmetry is normal, particularly in one-sided sports, and chasing perfect symmetry is not a useful goal.

Common programming questions

How much difference is too much?

Roughly ten percent is the usual threshold for attention. Below that, most people are simply normal.

Should I stop training the strong side?

No. Detraining the strong side is slower and costs you performance. Match its reps to the weaker side instead.

Will squats fix an imbalance?

Usually not. A bilateral lift lets the stronger side take more of the load, which is exactly how the gap persists unnoticed.

Safety and limitations

Stop if an exercise produces sharp pain, dizziness, or chest pain. A sudden, large asymmetry, or one that appeared with pain, needs assessment rather than corrective exercise.
